The administration called it the Interpreter.

It was built for patients who could still think but could no longer speak. Electrodes read neural activity; software turned it into words on a cheap LED screen.

It wasn't secret. For weeks, demonstrations made headlines worldwide: stroke victims speaking to families, locked-in patients answering questions.

Then one of those patients had a heart attack unexpectedly and died.

The Interpreter kept typing.

At first: names, places, fragments of apologies. We blamed residual activity in dead neurons.

As the technology improved:

Cold.

Dark.

I remember this.

Then one patient:

Don't send me back.

After that, nobody wanted the machine unplugged.

Families brought lawyers to deathbeds. Surgeons argued resuscitation had become cruelty. A nurse quit after a six-year-old lost his pulse in surgery and PLEASE PLEASE PLEASE filled the screen until they got him back.

Then Obstetrics wondered whether a newborn's brain was developed enough to read.

We tried once.

Seconds after delivery, the Interpreter displayed:

No.

Not again.

Someone photographed the screen, and it was leaked by morning.

Hospital entrances were blocked. Expectant mothers refused to enter maternity wards. Sterilization clinics were overwhelmed. Protesters shattered windows. Priests prayed outside through the night.

The government declared martial law, but it's too late.

I have always called my son's birth a miracle.

Now I only wonder what he was crying out for during it.
